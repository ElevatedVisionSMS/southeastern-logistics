Chile Truckers’ Strike 2026: What U.S. Seafood and Reefer Importers Need to Know

By Kelvin, Owner, Southeastern Logistics LLC · Updated November 12, 2026

Quick answer

Chile has been dealing with two trucker strikes back to back. Independent port truckers blocked container depots at the Port of San Antonio from October 28 to November 6, 2026, over diesel costs and new tolls. Then on November 10, a separate nationwide truckers’ strike started blocking highways and port access, including San Antonio, Valparaíso and Iquique. If you import refrigerated or frozen cargo from Chile, expect rolled bookings, bunched-up vessel arrivals and tighter dray capacity on this side over the next few weeks. Plan for it now, not when the boat shows up.

Key facts at a glance

  • Strike #1 (port): San Antonio independent truckers (Agreporsai), October 28 to November 6, 2026. Ended with an 8% freight rate increase, government-backed CORFO financing for small carriers and the right to bid directly on cargo contracts.
  • Containers stuck: By November 3, more than 3,000 containers couldn’t get into the port and about the same number couldn’t get out. Terminal storage hit roughly 90%.
  • Strike #2 (national): Started November 10, 2026, led by Fuerza del Norte, the No+TAG movement and other independent groups. Chile’s two biggest trucking confederations (CNTC and CNDC) did not join.
  • The demands: Lower fuel taxes, a fix to MEPCO (Chile’s fuel price stabilization fund) and cheaper highway tolls (TAG).
  • Government offer: A bill capping weekly fuel price increases at 20 pesos per liter for 90 days. Strikers rejected it. A 40 peso per liter increase hit on November 12.
  • Why it matters: Diesel in Chile is up about 53% this year while truck rates have been mostly frozen since 2021. That squeeze isn’t going away fast.

What happened at the Port of San Antonio?

San Antonio is Chile’s busiest container port, and roughly half of the country’s fruit exports leave through it. On the night of October 28, a group of independent owner-operators blocked the entrances to the seven off-port container depots that feed the STI and DP World terminals. Their beef: diesel kept climbing, rates didn’t, and four new electronic toll gates went up on Route 78, the highway into the port.

For nine days, boxes didn’t move. Empties piled up, loaded reefers sat and terminals ran over normal capacity. It hit right at the start of cherry season, Chile’s biggest fruit export. At least two of the express cherry vessels were moved to Valparaíso, and one sailed from San Antonio with only about half of its planned cherry cargo.

The truckers signed a deal on November 6 and San Antonio was reported back to normal operations. The backlog is a different story, and industry reporting says the effects will be felt through December.

Is the Chile truckers’ strike still going on?

As of November 11, 2026, yes. The second strike is national and it’s a different group. It started at midnight on November 10 with blockades on Route 5 (Chile’s main north to south highway), Route 68 between Santiago and Valparaíso, and port access roads including Iquique, San Antonio and Valparaíso. Talks with the Ministry of Transport ended without a deal on November 10. Strike leaders gave the government a couple of days to come back with something real and warned the blockades could get bigger.

The good news: the two largest trucking confederations stayed out of it, so this isn’t a full shutdown of Chilean trucking. The bad news: it doesn’t take a full shutdown to block a port gate.

Note: This situation is moving daily. Check the latest before you plan around it.

How does a strike in Chile affect U.S. importers?

A truck blockade 5,000 miles away still lands on your desk. Here’s how:

  • Rolled bookings. If a container can’t reach the terminal before cutoff, it misses the vessel and waits for the next one. That can mean a week or more.
  • Vessel bunching. When the backlog clears, cargo that should have spread out over two or three weeks shows up together. That’s when chassis, drivers and cold storage appointments get tight at Port Miami, Port Everglades and Savannah.
  • Schedule changes. Carriers shifting calls between San Antonio and Valparaíso can change transit times and ETAs.
  • Cost creep. Chilean truckers just won an 8% rate increase and diesel is still rising. Some of that will work its way into landed cost.
  • Free time pressure. A late, bunched arrival plus short free time is how a clean shipment turns into a demurrage bill.

For frozen seafood and other reefer cargo, the stakes are higher. Product is often sold before it’s even on the water, and your customer doesn’t care that a road in Chile was blocked. They care that it shows up on time and at temperature.

What should importers of Chilean reefer cargo do right now?

Here’s the short list:

  1. Call your forwarder or broker today. Ask which of your containers were affected, which are rolled and what the new ETAs are.
  2. Plan for a bunch-up. If you have multiple containers sailing in the next three to four weeks, assume some of them land together.
  3. Lock in your dray early. Line up trucking capacity before the backlog hits U.S. ports, not after.
  4. Know your free time. Know your last free day on every box and have a plan if a release comes in late.
  5. Have a backup for power and space. Gensets and yard space give you options if a cold storage appointment slips or the warehouse can’t take the box yet.
  6. Talk to your customers now. A heads up this week beats an apology next month.

Frequently asked questions

Why are truckers striking in Chile?

Rising diesel costs are the main reason. Diesel is up about 53% in 2026 while freight rates for many small operators have been mostly flat since 2021. Truckers also want lower fuel taxes, changes to the MEPCO fuel stabilization fund and cheaper highway tolls.

When did the San Antonio port strike end?

The independent truckers' blockade at San Antonio ended on November 6, 2026, after nine days. The deal included an 8% freight rate increase and financing support for small carriers.

Which Chilean ports are affected?

San Antonio was hit hardest by the first strike. The national strike that started November 10 has affected access to San Antonio, Valparaíso and Iquique.

Will the strike delay Chilean seafood and fruit shipments to the U.S.?

It can. Containers that missed vessel cutoffs get rolled to later sailings, and backlogs can lead to bunched arrivals at U.S. ports. Industry reports expect effects through December.

How can U.S. importers protect themselves from delays?

Get updated ETAs from your forwarder, book drayage early, track last free days closely and have backup options like gensets and yard space for refrigerated containers.

The bottom line

You can’t fix a blockade in Chile. You can make sure your side of the move is ready when the boxes finally show up. Just keep trucking.
